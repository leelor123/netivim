## 1. Summary (10 lines)

1. In the mid-19th century the land (part of Ottoman Syria, no single province named Palestine) was home to roughly 340,000-350,000 people, overwhelmingly Muslim Arabs, with Christian and small Jewish minorities (P-002).
2. By 1914 estimates range from about 616,000 (Ottoman census of three districts) to about 722,000 (McCarthy), with Jewish numbers in dispute between roughly 30,000 and 94,000 depending on method (P-002).
3. The Old Yishuv, a mostly religious Jewish community in Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed, lived on diaspora charity (halukka) and was a small share of the population (P-007).
4. The 1858 Land Code pushed landholders to register title; registration often favored merchants and notables over cultivators (P-018).
5. After the 1881 pogroms, Hibbat Zion societies promoted settlement; Rishon LeZion (1882) and Petah Tikva (1878, refounded 1883) began the First Aliyah (P-001, P-015, P-016).
6. Baron Edmond de Rothschild's funding stabilized many colonies (P-001, P-015).
7. Herzl's First Zionist Congress (Basel, 29-31 Aug 1897) adopted the Basel Program (P-003, P-004).
8. Arab voices responded early: Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi's 1899 letter, then newspapers Al-Karmil (1908) and Filastin (1911) (P-008, P-009, P-010, P-011).
9. The Second Aliyah brought about 35,000 arrivals (1904-1914), Hashomer (1909), Degania and Tel Aviv (both 1909) (P-014, P-006).
10. World War I brought deportations, famine and conscription to all communities; British forces took Beersheba and then Jerusalem in late 1917 (P-023, P-024, P-025, P-026).

## 3. Key people, places, organizations (codex candidates)

**Arab Muslim and Christian villagers and townspeople (עם ערבי מוסלמי ונוצרי, כפריים ועירוניים)**
Well over 80 percent of the population in the 1850s were Muslim, about 11 percent Christian (Scholch's rough breakdown) (P-002). Most lived in villages, some on land registered to absentee owners after 1858 (P-018). Codex note: a village page should show ordinary life (fields, wells, markets), never only "victims" or "opponents". Peasant voices are thin in the sources here; a Palestinian-history source (e.g., Tamari's diary-based work, P-030) should supply texture [needs pass 2].

**Old Yishuv (היישוב הישן)**
Jewish communities of Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed, with smaller groups in Jaffa, Haifa, Acre and Peki'in; both Sephardi/Mizrahi (including long-established Arabic-speaking Jews) and Ashkenazi. Many relied on halukka donations from abroad. About 24,000 Jews lived in the country in 1882 by one count (P-007).

**Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi (יוסף דיא אל-ח'אלידי)**
Jerusalem notable, mayor of the city in three terms and member of the first Ottoman parliament (P-008). On 1 March 1899 he wrote to Chief Rabbi Zadok Kahn asking that Zionists leave Palestine alone; Herzl replied on 19 March asking who would think of sending the non-Jewish population away (P-008, P-009). Rashid Khalidi, his descendant, reads the letter as an early warning and Herzl's reply as dismissive (P-009). Birth/death dates are inconsistent across sources (1842-1906 in P-008, flagged citation needed); P-029 gives different dates and should be ignored.

**Najib Nassar (נג'יב נצאר)**
Born 1865 in Lebanon, died 1947 in Nazareth. Founded Al-Karmil in Haifa in December 1908; opposed Zionist settlement and land sales while remaining loyal to the Ottoman state and calling himself a Palestinian patriot (P-010). Rashid Khalidi calls him a pioneer of Palestinian and Arab journalism (P-010).

**Filastin (פלסטין, עיתון)**
Jaffa newspaper founded 1911 by cousins Issa and Yousef al-Issa, Arab Christians; critical of Zionism and of Greek clerical dominance in the Orthodox church; suspended many times by authorities (P-011).

**Hovevei Zion / Hibbat Zion (חיבת ציון)**
Societies formed 1881 after Russian pogroms; Pinsker chaired the network named at Kattowitz in 1884; the Odessa Committee was approved in 1890 (P-016).

**Rishon LeZion (ראשון לציון)**
Founded 1882 by ten Hovevei Zion from Kharkiv led by Zalman David Levontin; called the first settlement of the New Yishuv (P-016). The role of Bilu pioneers versus Hovevei Zion is itself a point of historiographic argument (P-016).

**Petah Tikva (פתח תקווה)**
"Mother of the Moshavot", founded 1878, hit by malaria, abandoned and refounded 1883. Land was bought from two Christian businessmen of Jaffa; about thirty tenant farmers from the Arab village of Mulabbis worked it (P-015). The fate of those tenants is not in the source [UNVERIFIED; needs Mandel/Khalidi or Israeli archive].

**Zikhron Ya'akov (זכרון יעקב)**
Founded 1882; after financial trouble its assets passed to Baron Rothschild in 1883 (P-001).

**Baron Edmond de Rothschild (הברון אדמונד דה רוטשילד)**
Funded settlements, bought land and orchards/vineyards, improving colony economies (P-001; swamp drainage at Petah Tikva P-015).

**Theodor Herzl (תיאודור הרצל) and the First Zionist Congress (הקונגרס הציוני הראשון)**
Herzl convened and chaired the Congress in Basel, 29-31 August 1897; Max Nordau was elected first vice-president (P-004). The Basel Program sought a home for the Jewish people in Palestine "secured under public law" (P-003).

**Bar-Giora and Hashomer (בר-גיורא, השומר)**
Bar-Giora, a secret group of Second Aliyah pioneers, met first on 28 Sep 1907 in Jaffa (P-005 [W-only]); Hashomer was established in April 1909 at Mesha (Kfar Tavor) under Israel Shochat, Israel Giladi and Mendel Portugali, to win the right of Jews to guard and work in the settlements instead of non-Jewish guards (P-032, confirming P-006). Size is disputed: about 40 members in 1914 plus 50-60 candidates and auxiliaries, up to roughly 300 men at harvest time (P-032), versus "at most 100" members in a decade (P-006 [W-only]). Members spoke Arabic and wore mixed Arab and Circassian dress (P-032). Critics in the Jewish labor movement feared it could antagonize Arabs (P-032). Segev (2009) calls the image of Hashomer as peacemakers with the Arab population "ostensible" and a myth to be tested (P-033, opening paragraphs only). The claim that Hashomer raided Arab villages and displaced Arab watchmen rests only on P-006 and a search snippet [UNVERIFIED].

**Second Aliyah (העלייה השנייה)**
Around 35,000 arrivals 1904-1914, mostly from the Russian Empire, with many leaving again; Ben-Gurion is reported to have estimated 90 percent despaired and left (P-014; this is reported speech, verify against Ben-Gurion before use).

**Beersheba (באר שבע)**
An Ottoman-founded town and sub-district in the northern Negev, ordered in 1899 and developed from 1900 as a preplanned town with a grid, town hall, park, government house, Friday mosque and boarding school (P-017, P-037). Official accounts presented it as an Ottoman project aimed at the Bedouin, yet most engineers, builders, merchants and administrators were Arab Palestinian urbanites (P-037). An Israeli study of Ottoman sources argues officials, who saw the Bedouin as "ignorant" and "wild", gave them leniency and special treatment to bring them into the state (P-038). Bedouin-authored voices are still missing (Aref Abu-Rabia, cited by P-017, not read) [needs pass 3].

**Druze communities (הדרוזים)**
Villages such as Daliyat al-Karmel, Isfiya and mixed Peki'in on Carmel and in Galilee (P-020). The source is thin on the Ottoman period; Druze-authored history should be added [needs pass 2].

**Jaffa-Jerusalem railway (מסילת הברזל יפו-ירושלים)**
Opened 1892, about 86.5 km, 3.5-6 hours; a large civil-engineering project of the time (P-019).

**American Colony in Jerusalem (המושבה האמריקאית)**
Founded 1881 by Horatio and Anna Spafford; charitable work for Muslims, Jews and Christians; photographers whose archive (Matson collection) is at the Library of Congress (P-021, P-022).

## 5. Perspectives

**Accepted facts first.**
- The land was Ottoman territory with a largely Arab, Muslim population and Christian and Jewish minorities (P-002).
- Jewish immigration grew from 1882; the Zionist movement was formally organized at Basel in 1897 (P-001, P-004).
- Some Arab notables and journalists objected to Zionist settlement from the 1890s (P-008, P-010, P-011).
- WWI brought hardship to all communities (P-024, P-025).

**Arab Muslim and Christian narrative (in its own terms).**
The land was home: families, lineages, village fields and orchards, shrines and churches. Palestinian historiography (Khalidi) describes a society integrated in the Ottoman state, with educated notables who served it, and a national consciousness that grew in the early 20th century alongside Arabism, religion and local loyalties, "never exclusive" (P-009, P-012). Newspaper editors like Nassar and the al-Issas warned that Zionist settlement and land purchases threatened the majority population (P-010, P-011). Khalidi frames the 1899 letter as a plea to leave an inhabited country alone (P-009).

**Jewish / Zionist narrative (in its own terms).**
Jewish communities had lived in the four holy cities for centuries and prayed for return (P-007). After the 1881 pogroms, Hovevei Zion saw settlement as refuge and national renewal (P-016). Herzl stated that his movement meant benefit for the Ottoman state and the local population (as in his 19 March 1899 reply, P-008, P-009). The Second Aliyah pioneers saw labor and self-defense (Hashomer) as ways to stand on their own (P-014, P-006).

**Old Yishuv narrative.**
A religious community that did not primarily see itself as part of a political project; critics later called halukka dependency-promoting (P-007). Its relationship to the new Zionist settlers was complex (not covered in these sources [needs pass 2]).

**Druze and Bedouin.**
Both communities appear mostly as the subject of others' writing in these sources (P-017 via a Bedouin historian, P-020). The game should say "limited sources" rather than invent a voice.

**Ottoman state.**
Reform era (Tanzimat) with land registration, railways and new administrative towns (P-018, P-019, P-017), and it restricted Jewish immigration from 1882 (P-001).

## 9. "Did You Know?" facts (8)

1. A Jerusalem Jewish entrepreneur, Yosef Navon, won the concession for the 1892 railway to Jerusalem (P-019).
2. Beersheba was planned by the Ottomans as a grid town; one scholar calls it "the first Bedouin city" (P-017).
3. The Hovevei Zion settlers first wanted the Achor Valley near Jericho, but the sultan cancelled that purchase; they took the name Petah Tikva ("gateway of hope") to symbolize their aspirations (P-015).
4. Petah Tikva's land was sold by two Christian businessmen from Jaffa (P-015).
5. Ottoman officials presented Beersheba as an Ottoman project for the Bedouin, but most of its engineers, builders, merchants and administrators were Arab Palestinian urbanites (P-037).
6. Filastin was suspended more than 20 times by Ottoman and British authorities (P-011 [W-only]; upgrade before shipping).
7. In Ottoman Palestine 1915 is remembered as "the year of the locusts" (P-034).
8. The American Colony in Jerusalem ran a soup kitchen in WWI and its photographers' collection is now at the Library of Congress (P-021).

## 10. Sensitivity notes and what to avoid

- **No invented quotes.** Only the two documented lines (Herzl diary 3 Sep 1897, P-004; Herzl reply, P-008/P-009) are short enough to quote, and only with ID. Everything else is paraphrase. Do not put sentences in the mouths of al-Khalidi, Herzl or Nassar.
- **Do not frame the Arab population as an obstacle, or Jewish settlers as only colonizers or only victims.** Present motives and constraints on each side (P-009, P-016).
- **Population numbers:** always show a range with the reporter's name; never use a single figure to imply who "had more right".
- **Hashomer:** do not glorify or demonize; the displacement of non-Jewish guards is the stated aim of the organization (P-032); reported reprisals remain [UNVERIFIED] and must not be shown as fact. Segev's challenge to the "peacemaker" image (P-033) should shape the tone.
- **Mission design:** no shooting or harming people. The night-watch mission is observation and alerting only.
- **Unnamed individuals:** use fictional composites, labeled FICTIONAL COMPOSITE, for villagers, tenants and settlers.
- **Language:** use "Palestinian Arabs"/"Arab villagers" and "Jewish settlers"/"Old Yishuv" in context; avoid terms like "natives" or "pioneers" as a bare label.
- **Anachronism:** the word "Palestine" and the idea of a Palestinian national identity were developing in this period (P-012); do not project 20th-century national conflict backward onto 1850s villagers. Equally, do not claim Zionism had a finished plan for a state in 1882.
- **Unverified items:** Ottoman war entry date, famine death toll, Nili/Aaronsohn details (P-024 only mentions in passing), the fate of Mulabbis tenants. Leave out until sourced.
- **Sources that must be added before release:** Shapira (P-028), Mandel (P-013), Khalidi 1997 (P-012), Tamari (P-030), and archival primary text from the Central Zionist Archives and Israel State Archives; Druze, Bedouin and Old Yishuv voices.
