## 1. Topic-by-topic perspectives

### T1. Ottoman-era land sales and tenant displacement (Prologue, 1880s-1914; carries into Ch1)

**Palestinian narrative in its own terms (P-101, P-103):** Zionist colonization is described as running ahead of, and around, Ottoman restrictions. Walid Khalidi writes that the Ottoman authorities "repeatedly tried to legislate controls" on Zionist immigration and land acquisition but were frustrated by European pressure, corrupt local officials, "the greed of individual landowners" and use of the Capitulations system (P-101). The earliest tensions are traced to the colonizing program and its declared political aims, with "vast estates" bought by central Zionist institutions "from feudal absentee landlords in Beirut, over the heads of Palestinian tenants and sharecroppers" (P-101). Palestinians did not accept this passively: Khalidi's chronology lists Tiberias peasants alarmed at land acquisition (1901), an Ottoman governor of Jerusalem reporting Zionist evasion of land-transfer rules (1908), Palestinian deputies raising the issue in the Ottoman Parliament (1911, 1912), the founding of al-Karmil (1908) and Filastin (1911), and a 1913 telegram from 150 Jaffa Palestinians demanding measures (P-101). Beska (P-103) confirms from the Arabic press that after the 1908 Young Turk Revolution more than 30 newspapers appeared in Palestine and that "hundreds of articles" on Jewish immigration, land purchases and Zionism were written by Palestinians and other Arabs.

**Important internal nuance, voiced by Palestinian-side authors themselves:** the sellers were often large landowners, many of them not Palestinian residents. Hadawi (PLO Research Center, quoting Zionist land expert Granott) lists large sellers by name and family, including the Sursock family of Beirut (240,000 dunums in Marj ibn Amer, with a footnote citing the Shaw report that this sale displaced 1,746 farming families, 8,730 persons) (C1-102). Kanafani adds that "in many cases, it was feudal elements who bought the land to sell it to Jewish capital", i.e. resident Palestinian landlords also sold (C1-103). The game should therefore not present "Palestinians sold land" or "land was stolen" as simple facts; both are partial.

**Figures (who reports what):**
- Share of JNF/PICA/PLDC acquisitions to end of 1936 by seller type (Granott's tabulation, quoted by Hadawi): 52.6% from large absentee owners (358,974 dunums), 24.6% from large resident owners, 13.4% from government, churches and foreign companies, 9.4% from fellaheen (64,201 dunums); total tabulated 681,978 dunums, which Granott says is 55.4% of all Jewish acquisitions (C1-102). Granott is a Zionist land expert, quoted by a Palestinian author; both sides use the same numbers.
- Kanafani, citing a different table: 1920-22 purchases 75.4% from absentee, 20.8% resident, 3.8% small peasants; 1933-36 purchases 62.7% resident landlords, 14.9% absentee, 22.5% small peasants (C1-103). This shows the pattern changed over time; the two tables should not be merged.
- Jewish population in 1914: Khalidi gives "about eighty thousand", and says most kept European nationalities; about thirty Zionist colonies founded 1880s-1914 (P-101). Compare the range already in SOURCES.md (P-057, Gorny/McCarthy, not opened).

**Old Yishuv and Bedouin in this period:** see section 3.

**Two Lenses draft (T1):**
- Lens A (Zionist/Israeli): Land was bought legally, often at high prices, from owners who wanted to sell, in a country with large uncultivated areas; the settlers developed it.
- Lens B (Palestinian): Land was sold by absentee owners over the heads of tenants who had worked it for generations; the tenants lost their homes and livelihoods, and Arab newspapers and deputies warned about it from the 1890s.
- Shared ground: Large landowners, including some who lived in Beirut or Damascus, sold much of the land; tenants were not the sellers; both Palestinian and Zionist authors cite the same land-purchase tables.

### T2. Balfour Declaration reception (Ch1, 1917-1922)

**Palestinian narrative:** The end of WWI "brought bitter disappointment and a pervasive sense of foreboding" as the Balfour Declaration and secret wartime agreements became known; the Declaration's term "non-Jewish communities" was resented because Palestinians were then the large majority (Khalidi: 92 percent) (C1-101). The Palestinians "categorically rejected" the claim that biblical-era association gave European Zionists political title over their homeland, and felt betrayed given Britain's wartime promises to Sharif Hussein (C1-101). They organized: Christian-Muslim associations, three national congresses between January 1919 and August 1922 that rejected the Declaration and demanded a halt to Zionist immigration and land transfer plus representative government, and delegations to London in 1921 and 1922 (C1-101). The King-Crane Commission (1919) reported depth of Palestinian fear of Zionism; Khalidi quotes its remark that Zionists "looked forward to a practically complete dispossession" of the existing inhabitants (C1-101; verify the King-Crane wording against the primary text before using in game). The Mandate text (July 1922, Articles 2, 4, 6) was approved "without the consent of the Palestinians" and viewed as "an Anglo-Zionist condominium" (C1-101). Annual commemorations: Khalidi's photo captions describe Palestinians marking Balfour Day in mourning (2 Nov 1929) and a general strike protesting Lord Balfour's visit to Jerusalem (chronology entry in the 1925 block, March) (C1-101).

**Figures:** Population share is Khalidi's 92% (C1-101); the factcheck register has Jewish share ranges (P-057, C1-059: 1922 total 757,000). Show a range, not one number.

**Two Lenses draft (T2):**
- Lens A: The Declaration recognized the Jewish people's historic tie to the land and promised a national home, a hope after centuries of persecution.
- Lens B: A European power promised a country's future to a third party, calling its Arab majority "non-Jewish communities", without asking them.
- Shared ground: The same text promised that "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities" would not be prejudiced (C1-001); each side says the other half of the promise was not kept.

### T3. April 1920 (Nebi Musa) and May 1921 (Jaffa)

**Palestinian narrative:** Khalidi presents both as protests driven by fear of Zionism and by non-fulfilment of promises of independence: April 1920 "disturbances break out in Palestine due to fears of Zionism and nonfulfillment of promises of independence", the British commission of inquiry attributing them to "disappointment" and "fear of economic and political subjection"; May 1921, "protests against Zionist mass immigration" with the Haycraft Commission confirming fear of the consequences of immigration (C1-101). Halabi's study of the Nebi Musa festival (abstract opened only) frames the festival as a ritual through which Palestinian leaders and the British shaped a conservative, anti-Zionist nationalism that avoided direct anger at the British (C1-107). A Palestinian-oriented chronology note: the mayor of Jerusalem, Musa Kazim al-Husayni, was removed in April 1920 for opposing pro-Zionist policy (C1-101).

**Figures (disputed; show who reports):**
- April 1920: Khalidi, 5 Jews killed and 200 wounded (C1-101). No Arab casualty figure in Khalidi. I did not open the Palin report or another source, so the commonly cited toll that includes Arab dead is UNVERIFIED here; the lead should pull it from a British primary source before the game states a range.
- May 1921: Khalidi, 46 Jews killed and 146 wounded (C1-101). The Haycraft Commission's own medical statistics (hospital-admitted) give 95 killed, 48 Arabs and 47 Jews, and 219 wounded, 73 Arabs and 146 Jews; the Principal Medical Officer estimates 385 casualties treated overall (95 killed, 290 wounded) (C1-106). Note the one-person difference (46 vs 47 Jewish dead) and that Khalidi omits the Arab dead, many of whom the Haycraft report attributes to police and military action (e.g. it records 3 Arabs killed in the Khedera (Hadera) attack dispersal) (C1-106).
- Haycraft's stated cause: Arab "discontent with, and hostility to, the Jews, due to political and economic causes, and connected with Jewish immigration"; immediate trigger was a clash between rival Jewish May Day demonstrations (C1-106 seen in text; wording from the commission's summary, confirm page before quoting).

**Two Lenses draft (T3):**
- Lens A: Armed mobs attacked Jews in Jerusalem (1920) and Jaffa (1921), including an immigrant hostel; it showed Jews could not rely on protection.
- Lens B: These were eruptions of fear and anger at British pro-Zionist policy and mass immigration, by a population with no representative government; Arabs also died, many at the hands of the British.
- Shared ground: British inquiries (Palin, Haycraft) tied the violence to Arab fear of immigration and loss of independence; casualties on both sides; both sides were left feeling unprotected.

### T4. 1929 (Western Wall / Buraq, Hebron, Safed)

**Palestinian narrative:** Khalidi: the status quo at the Wall (customary arrangements between Jews and Muslims) was challenged by Jewish religious leaders in September 1928, then by an August 1929 demonstration by Revisionist Zionists, which was "perceived as evidence of Zionist designs on the mosques" of the Haram al-Sharif; this coincided with the enlargement of the Jewish Agency. Khalidi's caption says many Jews and Palestinians were killed, "the latter mostly at the hands of the British" (C1-101). He cites the Shaw Commission's conclusion that the fundamental cause was "disappointment of their political and national aspirations and fear for their economic future", and the Hope Simpson finding that there was no further land for new Zionist agricultural immigrants (C1-101). Land: Zionist land ownership rose from 2.04% (1919) to 4.4% (1929) of the country; Jewish population share 9.7% to 17.6% (Khalidi's figures) (C1-101).

**Cohen (Israeli historian, documenting Arab perspectives):** Per Ran Greenstein's review of Hillel Cohen's book, Cohen finds "no solid evidence" that Hajj Amin al-Husayni incited or directed the rioters; rumors of Jewish atrocities against Arabs fueled anger, especially in rural areas; many Palestinians protected Jewish neighbors; Jews also killed Palestinians but on a smaller scale; the review insists the context does not justify the violence (C1-104, reviewer's summary, the book itself not opened).

**Hebron specifically (Old Yishuv voice):** JVL account: violence 23-26 Aug; main killings Saturday 24 Aug; 67 Jews killed (12 Sephardi, 55 Ashkenazi); 22 Arab families sheltered Jewish neighbors; the Jewish community there was largely non-Zionist and long integrated with Arab neighbors, Sephardim Arabic-speaking; Britain evacuated 484 survivors including 153 children to Jerusalem; Hebron was left without a Jewish community for the first time in centuries (C1-105; pro-Israel aggregator, so treat the sub-claims such as the Slonim anecdote as that source's account).

**Figures (range, who reports):**
- Total deaths, nationwide: 133 Jews killed, 339 wounded (Khalidi, C1-101; Anglo-American Committee, C1-059); 116 Arabs killed (C1-059, per factcheck); Shaw Commission wounded figure 198 (prior pass, not reopened). Khalidi gives Jewish figures only.
- Hebron: 67 Jews killed (C1-105); the factcheck's AACI figure is "more than 60" (C1-059). Show "about 60-70".
- A Palestinian-oriented count of 113 Jews killed (prior pass) was not reopened: UNVERIFIED.

**Two Lenses draft (T4):**
- Lens A: A dispute over prayer at the Western Wall became a country-wide massacre; in Hebron an ancient, mostly non-Zionist Jewish community was destroyed.
- Lens B: Fear that the status quo at the Haram al-Sharif / al-Buraq was being changed, together with rumors and economic fear, set off the violence; Palestinian dead were mostly killed by British forces.
- Shared ground: Jews and Arabs were killed; some Arab families saved Jewish neighbors; the British Shaw Commission blamed Arab fear for livelihoods and political hopes; Hebron's Jewish community did not return.

### T5. The 1936-39 Great Revolt and its suppression

**Palestinian narrative (C1-101, C1-103):** In April 1936 National Committees formed in every town and larger village; the Arab Higher Committee (five parties) formed under Haj Amin al-Husayni; on 8 May a conference of the committees called for civil disobedience and a general strike under the cry "No taxation without representation" (C1-101). The strike ran May-October 1936 and "brought commercial and economic activity in the Palestinian sector to a standstill"; villagers took up arms; the brunt of fighting was borne by Palestinians; neighbouring Arab rulers asked the AHC to end the strike, which it did on 11 October 1936 (C1-101, C1-103). Kanafani gives a class reading: the revolt's mass base was peasants (citing Barbour that 90% of rebels were peasants), the leadership was mainly from notable families, and the revolt began from below (C1-103). Antecedents: Sheikh Izz al-Din al-Qassam, killed in November 1935 with about 25 men, after which his funeral drew large crowds (C1-103; Khalidi says the Qassam events "triggered" the revolt, C1-101 caption).

**British suppression, as the Palestinian narrative reports it:** Khalidi: after the 1937 Nazareth assassination the British outlawed the AHC and all parties, arrested leaders and exiled five to the Seychelles, held thousands in detention camps, used aircraft, tanks and artillery, held summary military trials including death for possessing arms, and imposed collective punishment (blowing up residential quarters, closing schools, fining, billeting). Jewish Settlement Police grew to 14,000 by early 1939; Special Night Squads (from June 1938) operated against Palestinian villages; Irgun introduced bombs in crowded markets (C1-101). Kanafani details the Jaffa old-city demolitions of June 1936 (about 220 houses, 6,000 homeless) (C1-103).

**Figures (disputed; always attribute):**
- Palestinians killed in action: Khalidi, at least 1,000 in 1938 alone ("conservatively") and at least 1,200 in the third phase (autumn 1938 to summer 1939); executions 54 (1938) and 55 (third phase); detained 2,463 in 1938, "more than twice" that the next year; Khalidi states Palestinians "lost some four thousand lives" fighting partition 1937-1939 (C1-101).
- Kanafani: about 1,000 killed by the stage of the League of Nations British estimate; more than 5,000 houses demolished, about 2,000 long sentences, 148 hanged at Acre, more than 5,000 imprisoned (C1-103). The 148 hangings differ sharply from Khalidi's 54 + 55; flag as an unresolved discrepancy between two Palestinian-side authors (the periods may not match, and I did not verify Kanafani's source, a footnote in his text).
- Jerusalem Story (Doha Institute affiliated site, no author, no sources cited): 3,832 Palestinians killed and over 14,000 injured (C1-108). This number also appears in prior-pass pages (C1-045, C1-047) and, per the factcheck, differs from the Anglo-American Committee: 1,791 verified deaths plus about 2,000 rebel estimated (C1-059). Search snippets attribute 3,832 to a Khalidi recalculation of British statistics; I could not open that source, so present 3,832 only as "reported by Palestinian-oriented sources".
- British/Jewish side deaths: Khalidi's captions mention 37 soldiers and police killed (official, early phase) (C1-101). A 415 Jewish deaths total appeared in a search snippet only: UNVERIFIED.

**Two Lenses draft (T5):**
- Lens A: A violent Arab uprising targeted Jews and the British, undermining the Jewish community's safety and prompting Jewish self-defense and British restrictions.
- Lens B: A popular anti-colonial revolt, led largely by peasants, against British rule, mass immigration and land loss; the British answered with mass detention, demolitions, hangings and collective punishment.
- Shared ground: Thousands of Palestinians and hundreds of Jews and British personnel died; the revolt defeated Palestinian leadership (exile, banning) which weakened them for 1947-48; numbers differ widely by source and are shown as ranges.

### T6. Tower-and-stockade (Homa u-Migdal) settlements, 1936-39

**What I could and could not find:** I did not open any Palestinian-authored account of tower-and-stockade settlements specifically (searches returned only Israeli heritage and aggregator pages; the existing C1-064/065 remain the only sources). Palestinian-narrative context that bears on it, all from opened sources:
- Land transfer was the core grievance of the national congresses and the revolt (C1-101, C1-103).
- The Palestinian reading of the JNF is that land bought becomes "inalienably Jewish" and only Jewish labour employed on it (P-101: JNF 1901 aims); the Peel partition proposal worried Palestinians because JNF rules would be applied to land in the Jewish state (C1-101).
- Kanafani lists Wadi al-Hawarith and Shatta among cases where "land was seized by Zionists after having evicted its inhabitants" (C1-103) and notes Kfar Haroeh was built on Hawarith land in 1934 (C1-110, Zochrot).
- Hadawi's note on British land restrictions (1940) is the other side of the same story: the 1940 Land Transfer regulations barred Jewish purchase in certain zones, and Jewish authorities used non-Jewish brokers to buy in prohibited zones (C1-102, footnote 11).

**Two Lenses draft (T6):**
- Lens A: Within a day, a fenced settlement went up on land the JNF had already bought, giving defense during the revolt and securing borders.
- Lens B: Each settlement was one more step in turning bought land into Jewish-only land, creating facts before any partition and often after tenants were removed.
- Shared ground: The sites were on land legally purchased from sellers who included absentee owners; the British permitted them for a time and then restricted purchases in 1939-40. (Gap: no Palestinian-authored account yet.)

### T7. Peel Commission partition proposal, July 1937

**Palestinian narrative (C1-101):** The Peel report itself named the underlying causes as the Arab "desire for independence" and "hatred and fear of the establishment of the Jewish national home", yet proposed partition into a Jewish state, a Palestinian state to be merged with Transjordan, and British enclaves. Palestinians "could not accept the legitimization of a Zionist political title"; hundreds of villages would fall within the Jewish state and hundreds of thousands of Palestinians become a subject minority; the Jewish state would get about 33% of the country, including all-Arab-owned Galilee, when Jews owned about 5.6%; and the Peel recommendation of forcible transfer "if necessary" was what horrified them most. Khalidi quotes Peel's own question whether the loss from partition is more than "Arab generosity can bear" (C1-101). The Bludan pan-Arab congress of September 1937 endorsed rejection; the Woodhead Commission (1938) concluded partition was not practicable (C1-101).

**Figures:** 33% of area to Jewish state vs 5.6% Jewish land ownership (Khalidi, C1-101); Hadawi's 1945 figure is 5.67% (C1-102), consistent. Peel's own text: check C1-058 (opened in prior pass) for the exact area and transfer language before the game states them.

**Two Lenses draft (T7):**
- Lens A: Partition was a compromise: a small state for Jews, with the British admitting the Mandate could not work; the Zionist leadership accepted the principle (with reservations).
- Lens B: Partition awarded a third of the land to a community owning about 6%, proposed moving Arabs out, and was imposed without consent; the Arab Higher Committee rejected it.
- Shared ground: The commission itself said both peoples' claims were irreconcilable; the plan was shelved in 1938 after the Woodhead Commission found it impracticable.

### T8. 1939 White Paper reception

**Palestinian narrative (C1-101):** The White Paper (May 1939) limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, then subject to "Arab acquiescence"; restricted land transfers in some zones; and envisaged an independent unitary state after ten years conditional on relations. Khalidi writes that "many Palestinians were positively impressed" but could not accept it because of the ambiguity of "Arab acquiescence" and the conditional independence. The Zionist side accused Britain of appeasement; he says it ended the Anglo-Zionist entente begun by Balfour. The London Conference (7 Feb-27 Mar 1939) failed; the British vetoed Haj Amin's participation (C1-101).

**Gap:** Statements of the Arab Higher Committee or the Mufti's rejection appeared only in search snippets (not opened). Do not present "the Palestinians rejected the White Paper" as simple fact: Khalidi's account is more nuanced (positive impression, rejection on specific points).

**Two Lenses draft (T8):**
- Lens A: With the Holocaust approaching, Britain closed the gates; the Jewish community saw it as betrayal.
- Lens B: A partial concession to Palestinian demands (limits on immigration and land sales), but with conditions that left the future in doubt; its terms were ambiguous.
- Shared ground: Neither side accepted it fully; it shaped Jewish illegal immigration and the Yishuv's break with Britain.

### T9. The 29 November 1947 partition vote

**Palestinian/Arab narrative (C1-101):** Khalidi's account: the UN recommended partition into a Jewish state, a Palestinian state and a special international regime for Jerusalem with economic union; "the Palestinians and other Arabs were as stunned as the Zionists... were jubilant." Palestinians saw partition as Zionist in provenance (the plan resembled the Zionist plan endorsed by Truman in August 1946); the vote of 33-13-10 was "largely due to" US pressure; no African or Asian state voted for except Liberia and the Philippines; India, Pakistan, Turkey and Afghanistan voted against; China abstained. The Jewish state would be larger than the Palestinian state (5,500 vs 4,500 sq mi) when Jews were no more than 35% of the population and owned under 7% of the land; within the proposed Jewish state Jews owned about 600 of 5,500 sq mi; the proposed Jewish state would include nearly all citrus land (equally owned), 80% of cereal land (entirely Arab-owned) and 40% of Palestinian industry; Jaffa cut from its hinterland; hundreds of villages separated from communal fields. The Arabs asked that the ICJ be consulted on the General Assembly's competence to enforce partition against the majority; that draft was defeated 21-20 in the Ad Hoc Committee, and a related proposal on sharing the Jewish refugee burden failed 16-16 with 25 abstentions. Palestinian and Arab argument: "why pay for the Holocaust" committed in Europe by Europeans, and Zionism predated it (C1-101). Consequently, they felt not bound by a non-binding recommendation.

**Figures (who reports):**
- Vote 33-13-10 with one absent: C1-060 (UN record) and Khalidi (C1-101) agree.
- Jewish share of population ("no more than 35 percent", Khalidi) vs UN/UNSCOP data in factcheck (not reopened by me).
- Land ownership: Jews under 7% (Khalidi); 5.67% (April 1945, Hadawi from Mandate tax records) (C1-102); Granott (Zionist land expert, quoted by Hadawi): about 1,850,000 dunums in Jewish possession at June 1947, of which 181,100 by government concession, leaving a net 1,668,900 dunums (C1-102). By my arithmetic on Hadawi's totals, 1,850,000 dunums is about 7% of Palestine. Hadawi stresses the land survey was taxation-oriented and Mandate title settlement covered only about 5% of the country by 1948 (C1-102).
- The Arab Higher Committee statements at the UN (Jamal Husseini, 29 Sep 1947) exist in UN press releases; I could not open the text (page truncated). Not used.

**Two Lenses draft (T9):**
- Lens A: The UN offered a state to Jews and one to Arabs; the Jewish leadership accepted, the Arab side refused and war followed.
- Lens B: A General Assembly recommendation gave the majority-minority Jewish side more than half the land and left a large Arab minority in it; Arab states and the AHC argued it violated self-determination and asked for an ICJ opinion, which was voted down.
- Shared ground: The vote was 33-13-10; the resolution was a non-binding recommendation; the Jewish state would have had a large Arab minority and the Arab state almost no Jews.

## 3. Bedouin, Druze and Old Yishuv perspectives found

**Bedouin**
- Ahmed Amara (Palestinian citizen of Israel, legal scholar), Journal of Levantine Studies 2017 (OPENED, web page): under Ottoman law Bedouin rights arose from cultivation and tax payment, recognised through sheikh-kept registers; Bedouin cultivated large areas around Beersheba, mainly barley, for centuries; British land registration began 1928 but did not reach the Negev until 1948; Bedouin sometimes registered land in their own names before transferring to Zionist bodies or individuals; the British expected about 2 million dunums in the Beersheba district to be recognised as Bedouin-owned. The article is mainly about post-1948 law; use only the Ottoman/Mandate background and attribute it (P-105).
- Kanafani gives Bedouin numbers (66,553 in 1931; 103,000 in 1922) and claims they played a principal part in the 1929 and 1936 revolts (C1-103). The same text says Bedouin were "nearly 35% of the population", which contradicts his own counts (about 6-10% of Palestine's roughly 1 million); do not use the 35%.
- Wadi al-Hawarith (Hawarith tribe, a Bedouin-origin community per Zochrot's genealogy) is the best-known tenant-eviction case of the Mandate: land owned by the al-Tayyan family of Jaffa, sold to the JNF in 1929, tenants resisted eviction into the 1930s, Kfar Haroeh built 1934 (C1-110, C1-103). Zochrot is advocacy-oriented; sizes disagree (Kanafani 40,000 dunums vs about 30,000 in an index source, not citable). Scholarly follow-up not opened: Raya Adler (Cohen), "The tenants of Wadi Hawarith", IJMES 1988 (pointer only).
- Existing: P-037/P-038 (Beersheba, Bedouin and Ottoman civilizing mission; Israeli and German academics). Still missing: a Bedouin-authored source.

**Druze**
- Eduardo Aboultaif (Lebanese American University), Arab Studies Quarterly 40(3), 2018 (abstract OPENED, full text not): argues the Druze of Palestine backed the Palestinian Arab cause and took part politically and militarily (1929, 1936, 1948); credits Sheikh Amin Tarif with leading community opposition to Zionism; intended to counter narratives of Druze-Zionist cooperation (C1-109).
- The competing view (search snippets only, NOT OPENED): Druze elites were largely neutral and contacts with the Jewish community grew during the 1936-39 revolt, with a non-aggression understanding. I could not open a Druze-authored or Israeli-scholar source for that view. The game must show this as a contested question, not state either position as fact.

**Old Yishuv**
- Shanon Shah, Critical Dictionary of Apocalyptic and Millenarian Movements (2021 edition of a 2017 text) (OPENED): Agudat Israel founded 1912 to oppose Zionism; Edah Haredit formed 1935 by Haredim who thought Agudat Israel conceded too much; Neturei Karta split in Jerusalem in 1938 (note other sources give 1921; dates conflict, so avoid a firm date); Jacob Israel de Haan, Agudat Israel figure, negotiated with Emir Abdullah, shot dead in 1924 by a Haganah agent (the article's wording; the identity of his killers is asserted in the article, so attribute) (P-104).
- JVL on Hebron 1929 (C1-105): a largely non-Zionist, partly Arabic-speaking Sephardi and Ashkenazi Old Yishuv community, with Arab families who sheltered Jews; the community's leaders reportedly had declined Zionist security offers. This is the best voice for "Jews who were not part of the Zionist project, and who were targeted anyway".
- Not opened: Jacobson and Naor, Oriental Neighbors (Brandeis UP 2016) on Mizrahi/Sephardi Jews as intermediaries with Arab neighbors (catalog blurb only); existing P-054 (Halukkah) remains the Old Yishuv economic source.
